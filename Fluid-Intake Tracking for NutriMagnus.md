# Fluid-Intake Tracking for NutriMagnus

Oct 11, 2026 · @Thomas C Cloyd

## Summary

NutriMagnus should track **total water** (beverages plus food moisture), show beverage and food water separately, and compare totals against population reference values as rough guides, not prescriptions. The official adequate intakes are 3.7 L/day for men and 2.7 L/day for women in the U.S.¹ and 2.5 and 2.0 L/day in Europe.² Both are derived from typical intakes of healthy people, not measured physiological requirements.¹,²

- **Who benefits:** The strongest case is kidney-stone prevention, where a randomized trial cut 5-year recurrence from 27% to 12.1% with high water intake.⁴ Older adults come next, because about one in four non-hospitalised adults aged 65 and over has low-intake dehydration.⁸ Healthy, sedentary adults who drink to thirst gain little.¹,²³
- **What to count:** Both the U.S. Institute of Medicine (IOM) and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) define their values as total water, including food moisture.¹,² Food supplies roughly 19–20% of intake in the U.S.¹ and 20–30% in Europe.² Coffee, tea, and dilute alcoholic drinks hydrate about as well as water.³⁰,³¹,³³
- **Official targets:** See the table under Question 3. Clinical guidelines add targets for stone formers (urine ≥2.5 L/day),⁵,⁶ older adults (drinks ≥1.6 L women, ≥2.0 L men),⁹ and athletes (avoid losing more than 2% of body mass).¹⁸ The popular “8×8 glasses” rule has no scientific basis.²³

## How evidence strength is rated

This report uses a custom **Evidence Grade** (A–D) that I assigned from the study designs found during research. It rates how well the evidence supports *monitoring or changing fluid intake* for a group, not how important hydration is in general. It is a qualitative judgement, not a formal GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations) assessment; where a source gives its own formal grade, that is reported alongside.

| Grade | Meaning | Typical basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A – Strong** | Benefit shown in randomized trials with patient-relevant outcomes, preferably confirmed by systematic review; guidelines make firm recommendations | ≥1 adequately sized randomized controlled trial (RCT) plus systematic review or strong guideline |
| **B – Moderate** | Consistent experimental or physiological evidence, or a systematic review of mostly small or heterogeneous trials; clinical outcomes less certain | Meta-analyses of lab trials, small RCTs, high-quality prevalence meta-analyses |
| **C – Limited** | Mainly observational associations, single small trials, or expert consensus | Cohorts, cross-sectional studies, consensus statements |
| **D – Insufficient or negative** | Trials show no benefit, or there is essentially no evidence | Null RCTs, absent data |

## Question 1: Who benefits from fluid tracking?

Kidney-stone formers have the strongest evidence for counting intake; older adults have the strongest case for prompting. For most others, a tracker is a self-awareness tool rather than a therapeutic one. Individual needs vary widely: an isotope study of 5,604 people in 26 countries found average water turnover of 4.3 L/day in men and 3.4 L/day in women, rising with energy expenditure, body size, heat, and humidity.³

| Population | Key evidence | Study types | Grade |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Kidney-stone formers | Recurrence 12.1% vs 27% over 5 years with urine ≥2 L/day;⁴ guidelines target urine ≥2.5 L/day;⁵,⁶ about 15 fewer episodes per 100 people over 5 years⁷ | RCT, systematic review, guidelines | A |
| Older adults (≥65) | About 24% of non-hospitalised older adults have low-intake dehydration (serum osmolality >300 mOsm/kg);⁸ ESPEN (European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism) sets minimum drink targets;⁹,¹⁰ intervention trials to raise intake are weak⁹ | Prevalence meta-analysis, guideline | B (need); C (tracking) |
| Heart failure | Routine restriction losing support: FRESH-UP (Fluid REStriction in Heart failure vs liberal fluid UPtake) RCT (n = 504) found no harm from liberal intake and less thirst distress;¹³ meta-analyses conflict¹⁵,¹⁶ | RCT, meta-analyses, guidelines | B (against routine restriction) |
| Chronic kidney disease (CKD), stage 3 | Coaching to drink more did not slow decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) (CKD WIT \[Chronic Kidney Disease Water Intake Trial\] RCT)¹⁷ | RCT | D (drink more) |
| Athletes, heat, outdoor workers | Deficits >2% of body mass impair performance;¹⁸ over-drinking causes hyponatraemia¹⁹ | Position stands, consensus | B |
| Cognition | Small impairments at deficits >2% of body mass in one meta-analysis;²⁰ another found no significant effect;²² everyday dehydration linked only to poorer sustained attention²¹ | Meta-analyses, longitudinal study | B (only at ≥2% deficits) |
| Weight management | Extra water associated with 44–100% more weight loss vs control in small adult trials⁷ | Systematic review of small RCTs | C–B |
| Pregnancy and lactation | Higher reference values,¹,² but derived from intakes; no outcome trials found | Reference values | C |
| Healthy, sedentary adults | Thirst plus drinking with meals maintains hydration¹,²³ | Physiology, reference reports | Little benefit |

**Kidney stones.** Borghi et al.’s RCT enrolled patients after a first idiopathic calcium stone; time to recurrence was also longer with high water intake (38.7 vs 25 months).⁴ The American Urological Association (AUA) recommends fluid intake producing urine of at least 2.5 L/day (Standard; Evidence Strength Grade B), and often 4 L/day or more of intake for cystine stones.⁵ The European Association of Urology (EAU) gives a strong recommendation for urine above 2.5 L/day.⁶ The American College of Physicians (ACP) instead advises enough fluid to make at least 2 L of urine per day (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence).³⁷ Because these targets are urine output, intake must also cover sweat, breath, and faecal losses.¹

**Heart failure.** The 2021 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guideline says restriction to 1.5–2 L/day *may be considered* in severe heart failure (HF) or hyponatraemia, with no level of evidence assigned.¹¹ The 2022 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology/Heart Failure Society of America (AHA/ACC/HFSA) guideline rates its benefit as uncertain (Class IIb, Level C).¹² In FRESH-UP, the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) overall score was 74.0 with liberal intake vs 72.2 with restriction (adjusted difference 2.17; P = 0.06).¹³ A 2026 meta-analysis of 9 RCTs (n = 1,271) found lower all-cause mortality with restriction (risk ratio \[RR\] 0.54; 95% confidence interval \[CI\] 0.31–0.94) but no difference in readmissions; five of its nine trials were rated high risk of bias.¹⁵ A 2025 meta-analysis found restriction lowered intake without improving clinical outcomes.¹⁶ An ESC Heart Failure Association consensus defines normal intake as 1.5–2.5 L/day (15–30 mL/kg/day).¹⁴ For these patients, the tracker should follow the cardiologist’s limit, not set one.

**Kidney disease.** The CKD WIT trial (n = 631) found one-year eGFR change of −2.2 vs −1.9 mL/min/1.73 m² (adjusted difference −0.3; 95% CI −1.8 to 1.2).¹⁷ Patients on dialysis or with advanced CKD are often given explicit limits, which a tracker can help them follow.

**Athletes and heat.** The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) recommends preventing deficits greater than 2% of body weight, estimating sweat rate by weighing before and after exercise, and adding sodium for sessions over an hour.¹⁸ The 2015 hyponatraemia consensus identified overdrinking as the main cause of exercise-associated hyponatraemia and named drinking to thirst as the safest individual strategy.¹⁹ Sweat-rate logging around training is more useful here than a daily total.

**Risks.**

- *Overhydration:* The IOM set no upper limit for water but notes that acute water toxicity has occurred when intake outpaced the kidney’s maximum excretion rate of about 0.7–1.0 L/hour.¹
- *Thirst is not “too late”:* Thirst begins when blood concentration rises by less than 2%, well before clinical dehydration.²³
- *Compulsive tracking:* I found no studies on hydration-tracking compulsions. Daily goal-reinforcement could plausibly feed orthorexic or eating-disorder patterns, such as water-loading before weigh-ins. This is clinical reasoning, not a research finding.

## Question 2: Total water or beverages only?

Track total water, display beverage and food water separately, and compare each against the matching reference. The IOM adequate intake covers drinking water, all beverages, and water in food; it also publishes the beverage portion, about 81% of the total in U.S. survey data.¹ EFSA’s values likewise include food moisture.² ESPEN’s geriatric targets, by contrast, are for drinks only.⁹

**How much water comes from food.** Estimates vary by country and diet:

- U.S.: about 19–20% in IOM survey data;¹ an earlier U.S. analysis put food at about 28%.²⁴
- Europe: EFSA assumes 20–30%.² A survey of European national methods found only Ireland reported a figure, 33%,²⁵,²⁶ and cited about 40% for China.²⁵
- Diets high in fruit, vegetables, soups, and cooked grains raise the share, so a fixed 20% assumption would misstate intake for a produce-heavy diet.

**Metabolic water.** Oxidising food yields roughly 250–350 mL/day in sedentary adults.¹ Neither the IOM nor EFSA counts it in their intake values,¹,² so including it would inflate comparisons with targets.

**Food moisture data.** U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) nutrient databases list Water as nutrient #255, in g per 100 g of edible portion, for nearly every food.²⁷,²⁸ Water is a “proximate” component, and carbohydrate is calculated by difference from it, so the values are integral to the database.²⁷ Moisture is measured as weight lost on oven drying, so values carry small errors from other volatile compounds.²⁹ For NutriMagnus, 1 g of water can be treated as 1 mL; use cooked-state entries, since cooking changes water content.

**Coffee, tea, and alcohol.**

- The IOM states caffeinated and alcoholic beverages can count toward total water, because any diuretic effect may be transient.¹
- In a randomized crossover trial of 13 drinks (72 men, 1 L each), 4-hour urine output after coffee, tea, cola, lager, juice, and sports drink did not differ from water; milk and oral rehydration solution were retained better.³⁰ The trial was funded by the European Hydration Institute.
- In a crossover study of 50 habitual male coffee drinkers, four cups a day for 3 days produced no differences in hydration markers or total body water versus water.³¹ The coffee industry funded it.
- A meta-analysis of 16 studies found caffeine raised resting urine output by about 109 mL, with no effect during exercise.³²
- In older men, wine and spirits raised urine output over 4 hours but not over 24 hours; beer did not differ from non-alcoholic beer.³³ Three authors were affiliated with the Dutch Beer Institute.

**Design implication.** Count all beverages at their actual water content (juice and milk are about 85–90% water), optionally offer an adjusted view that discounts spirits, and label any adjustment as an approximation.

## Question 3: Official recommendations

Adults need about 2.7–3.7 L/day of total water by U.S. reference values¹ and 2.0–2.5 L/day by European ones.² Both are adequate intakes based on observed intakes of healthy populations at moderate temperature and activity.¹,²

EFSA beverage figures are calculated as 80% of total water, using EFSA’s own assumption that food supplies about 20%;² they are my derivation, not EFSA values. EFSA treats adolescents aged 14 and over as adults.²

**Other official and clinical guidance.**

- **IOM basis:** The values are median intakes from national survey data. The IOM states that intake below them may not confer added risk and should not be read as a specific requirement.¹ Neither the IOM nor EFSA scales its values to body weight. The main weight-based figure in clinical use, 25–30 mL/kg/day of water, comes from National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidance on routine intravenous maintenance for adults in hospital, not from dietary reference values.³⁸ Measured U.S. intakes in 2009–2012 averaged 3.46 L/day for men and 2.75 L/day for women.³⁴
- **World Health Organization (WHO):** No dietary reference intake for water. Its drinking-water guidelines assume 2 L/day for a 60 kg adult only to set contaminant limits.³⁵ Its water-supply guidance recommends a planning minimum of 5.3 L per person per day for drinking, sized for the highest-need group.³⁶
- **ESPEN (older adults):** Offer at least 1.6 L of drinks per day to older women and 2.0 L to older men, unless a clinical condition requires otherwise.⁹,¹⁰
- **ACSM (exercise):** Prevent body-mass loss greater than 2%, test individual sweat rates, and add sodium for exercise longer than an hour.¹⁸ Drink to thirst as the safest individual strategy.¹⁹
- **Kidney stones:** AUA and EAU target urine ≥2.5 L/day;⁵,⁶ the ACP targets urine ≥2 L/day.³⁷
- **Heart failure:** ESC suggests 1.5–2 L/day only in severe HF or hyponatraemia;¹¹ AHA/ACC/HFSA call restriction’s benefit uncertain;¹² an ESC consensus sets normal intake at 1.5–2.5 L/day.¹⁴

**The “8×8” rule.** A 2002 review found no scientific evidence for drinking eight glasses of water a day.²³ It traced the rule to a likely misreading of a 1945 recommendation of about 1 mL per kcal of food, which noted that most of that water is already in prepared food.²³ Eight 8-oz glasses equal about 1.9 L of beverages.

## Recommendations for NutriMagnus

These are design suggestions built on the evidence above; the safety rails and profile choices are my judgement, not research findings.

1. **Data model.** For each logged food and beverage, compute water in grams from nutrient #255 times grams consumed,²⁷,²⁸ tagged as beverage or food. Keep metabolic water as an optional derived field, excluded from target comparisons.¹
2. **Outputs.** Show daily totals and 7-day averages of total, beverage, and food water, plus the food share (%). Compare total water with both the IOM and EFSA values for the user’s age, sex, and life stage, and let the user choose which one sets their target. Offer a third, weight-based option of 25–30 mL/kg/day of total water, scored against water\_g like the IOM and EFSA figures. It comes from guidance for routine intravenous maintenance in hospitalised adults, so label it as a resting-patient minimum, not a target for healthy people.³⁸ Per-kilogram scaling misleads at both ends: water turnover rises only about 0.7 L/day per extra 50 kg of body weight,³ so a linear rule overstates needs for heavy users and understates them for light ones. NICE itself caps the figure at about 3 L/day and uses ideal body weight in obesity.³⁸ At 50 kg it gives only 1.25–1.5 L/day, below both the IOM and EFSA values. Show a warning whenever the weight-based figure falls below the user’s selected reference, and for users aged 65 and over also check drinks against the ESPEN minimum.⁹ Compare beverages with the matching beverage figure (IOM published, or EFSA derived at 80% of total) or the ESPEN drinks target for older adults.¹,²,⁹
3. **Opt-in profiles.**
   - Stone prevention: aim for urine ≥2.5 L/day, with optional urine-volume logging.⁵,⁶
   - Older adult: ESPEN drinks minimum of 1.6 L (women) or 2.0 L (men).⁹
   - Clinician-set limit: an upper cap for heart failure, dialysis, or hyponatraemia, with no “more is better” messaging.¹¹,¹⁴
   - Exercise and heat: sweat-rate calculator from pre- and post-exercise body mass, plus a drink-to-thirst reminder.¹⁸,¹⁹
4. **Safety rails.** Warn when intake exceeds about 1 L/hour, the approximate upper limit of kidney excretion.¹ Never show over-target intake as success. Default to weekly framing, and offer a non-numeric “thirst-guided” mode to reduce compulsive use.
5. **Protein link.** For high-protein plans, a note that extra urea raises obligatory urine water is reasonable; label it as physiological reasoning, since I found no outcome trials.

## Caveats

- Reference values are population medians; individual turnover varies severalfold with body size, activity, climate, and age.³
- Several beverage-hydration studies were industry-funded or industry-affiliated, and most enrolled only men.³⁰,³¹,³³
- The heart-failure meta-analyses reach opposite conclusions on mortality, and most included trials were small or at high risk of bias.¹⁵,¹⁶
- I read the Borghi trial’s results through secondary sources, not the full text.⁴
- Evidence on constipation and high-protein diets was not established here, and the guidance on compulsive tracking is clinical reasoning, not research.
